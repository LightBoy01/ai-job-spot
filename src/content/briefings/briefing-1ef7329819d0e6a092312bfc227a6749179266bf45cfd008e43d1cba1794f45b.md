### Summary\nStreet photographer Bruce Gilden, known for his close-up and often confrontational style, published a new book featuring his work from the UK across the 1970s and 2000s. Now in his seventies, Gilden reflects on his enduring practice of getting \"very, very close to his subjects\" to capture candid moments of life, particularly focusing on the \"underbelly\" of British society.\n\n### Why It Matters\nFor professionals in the AI space, Gilden's approach to street photography offers a compelling analog for critical data collection and ethical observation. His deliberate choice to get \"very, very close\" to subjects, often without explicit consent, immediately brings to mind discussions around privacy, surveillance, and the ethics of data acquisition in public spaces. As AI systems become increasingly sophisticated in capturing and interpreting visual data – from facial recognition to behavioral analytics – Gilden's work forces us to confront the inherent biases and perspectives embedded in any dataset. His focus on a specific societal \"underbelly\" illustrates how the 'lens' through which data is collected (whether a photographer's camera or an AI model's training data) fundamentally shapes the resulting representation. This underscores the vital importance of diverse, ethically sourced data and transparent methodologies to avoid perpetuating narrow or biased views of humanity within AI applications. Furthermore, Gilden’s intensely human, subjective interpretation of his subjects stands in stark contrast to algorithmic objectivity, prompting reflection on how AI can be trained to recognize nuance, context, and the complex social dynamics that a human artist instinctively captures, rather than simply categorizing or reducing individuals to data points. His art serves as a powerful reminder that robust AI development requires not just technical prowess, but a profound understanding of the human element and the ethical implications of observation.
