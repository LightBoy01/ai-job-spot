<img src="https://spectrum.ieee.org/media-library/glowing-stars-fill-a-pink-lined-cube-against-a-black-background-stars-spill-outside-the-cube.jpg?id=61623182&amp;width=2000&amp;height=1500&amp;coordinates=0%2C0%2C0%2C1"><br><br><p>We forge ahead with scientific quests,<br>but even zealous efforts hit a wall<br>trying to make what nature most detests:<br>nothing at all.</p><p>It seems like such a little thing to do—<br>removing every molecule. We find<br>that though we displace almost all, a few<br>remain behind.</p><p>It takes attentive planning and robust<br>equipment in a lab to do the chore<br>of pumping vacuum pressure down to just<br>a millitorr.</p><p>The stalwart researcher persists and loses<br>sleep, but can’t reach perfection—I’m afraid<br>the universe still stubbornly refuses<br>to be unmade.</p><p>Even in deepest space, a cubic meter<br>contains some particles. We must assess<br>there is no void, although conditions teeter<br>on emptiness.</p><p>The quantum mysteries will vex and weary<br>the brightest mind, the sharpest physicist.<br>True nothingness, while wonderful in theory,<br>does not exist.</p>
