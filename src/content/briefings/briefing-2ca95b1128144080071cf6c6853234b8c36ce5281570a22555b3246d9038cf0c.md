### Summary\\nThe article uses Henry Ford's \"faster horses\" analogy to emphasize the critical need for transformative thinking, particularly in customer service. It argues that merely optimizing existing, often failing systems through incremental improvements is insufficient and will not lead to true innovation. Instead, it subtly calls for a radical rethinking and overhaul, suggesting that what's truly needed is a completely new approach or solution, implicitly pointing towards advanced technologies like AI as the catalyst for such fundamental change.\\n\\n### Why It Matters\\nThis perspective is profoundly important for professionals in the AI space because it highlights a pervasive challenge in enterprise AI adoption: the tendency to apply advanced technology to outdated paradigms. For AI to deliver on its transformative promise, practitioners must actively steer organizations away from merely automating \"faster horses\" – i.e., optimizing inefficient, legacy customer service processes – and towards conceptualizing entirely new \"cars.\" This means leveraging AI's unique capabilities (e.g., proactive problem-solving, hyper-personalization, intelligent self-service, predictive analytics) to redefine the entire customer experience architecture from the ground up. AI professionals are not just implementers; they are architects of future systems. Their role involves challenging conventional thinking, identifying opportunities for disruptive innovation rather than incremental gains, and guiding stakeholders to envision processes and services that were previously impossible. Failing to adopt this \"car\" mentality risks underutilizing AI's potential, leading to marginal ROI, reinforcing the perception that AI is merely a cost-cutting tool, and ultimately missing the chance to create sustainable competitive advantage through truly novel customer experiences and operational efficiencies. It's a call to move beyond reactive problem-solving with AI to proactive experience design and strategic business model innovation.
